Desmopressin

Synthetic analogue of arginine vasopressin: 3-mercaptopropionic acid (deaminated Cys1)-Tyr-Phe-Gln-Asn-Cys-Pro-D-Arg-Gly-NH2, disulfide-bridged; contains a D-arginine and a C-terminal amide, so no standard one-letter sequence is given.

Non-standard residues or modifications: no plain one-letter sequence

Also: Desmopressin acetate, 1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin · Codes: DDAVP, SER120 · Brands: DDAVP, Nocdurna

A synthetic nine-residue analogue of the pituitary hormone vasopressin, modified to act mainly on kidney water reabsorption with little effect on blood pressure. It has been an approved prescription drug for decades in tablet, nasal, sublingual and injectable forms. Labelled uses include central diabetes insipidus, primary nocturnal enuresis, nocturia due to nocturnal polyuria, and, for the injectable form, certain bleeding disorders.

02What the human evidence shows

E1

Nocturia due to nocturnal polyuria in adults

3published human RCTs
3published human trials
29registered studies
10with posted results

Several adequately sized randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trials of low-strength sublingual and nasal formulations reported modest reductions in night-time voids compared with placebo. Effect sizes over placebo were small in absolute terms. Hyponatraemia was the principal safety concern and was more frequent in older participants.

  • [1] Greater reduction in nocturnal voids than placebo in men with nocturia in a randomised placebo-controlled trial
  • [2] Pooled analysis of two placebo-controlled phase 3 trials reported fewer nocturnal voids with the nasal formulation than with placebo

Registered-study counts: ClinicalTrials.gov intervention search “desmopressin nocturia”, fetched 2026-10-10. Registered studies include trials of other uses; the gap between registered and reported is itself a finding.

A Cochrane systematic review of randomised trials found that desmopressin reduced the number of wet nights compared with placebo while it was being taken, but the review found no evidence that the benefit persisted after treatment stopped. The exact number of included RCTs is not restated here.

  • [3] Fewer wet nights than placebo during treatment in children with nocturnal enuresis; no sustained effect after stopping

Use in central diabetes insipidus rests on early uncontrolled clinical studies from the 1970s showing an antidiuretic response, and on long clinical use. No placebo-controlled trial is cited here; in a condition with a direct physiological replacement rationale, randomised placebo-controlled trials have not been the basis of approval.

  • [4] Early clinical report of antidiuretic response to nasal desmopressin in patients with central diabetes insipidus

03Safety signals

S1Large long-term human exposure (post-marketing surveillance)
  • Hyponatraemia [6]
    A systematic review and meta-analysis found hyponatraemia to be a recognised risk in older adults treated with desmopressin for nocturia. Severe hyponatraemia can cause seizures.

04What is unknown

  • No randomised placebo-controlled trial with clinical outcomes underpins use in central diabetes insipidus; the evidence is historical and uncontrolled
  • Whether the small average reduction in night-time voids in nocturia trials outweighs hyponatraemia risk in people over 65 is not settled by the cited trials
  • Benefit in childhood enuresis after treatment stops was not demonstrated in the cited review
  • Status in the EU (national authorisations), UK, Australia, Japan and China was not verified from regulator sources for this record

07Mechanism

Desmopressin is a selective vasopressin V2 receptor agonist; in human studies it reduces urine output by increasing water reabsorption in the kidney collecting duct and raises circulating factor VIII and von Willebrand factor levels.

Mechanism is placed last on purpose: a plausible mechanism is not evidence that a compound works in people.
